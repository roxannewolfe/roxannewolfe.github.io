## Main Body
{% include note.html type="citation" text="The Turing test is a thought experiment intended to answer the question of whether computers can think, by replacing the more frequent problem of defining thought to a more practical question of how a machine would behave compared to a certain human in a specific game." note="Turing, 1950." %} {% include note.html type="citation" text="To critics, this question implicitly has an ‘other minds’ dimension as the relationship (or lack-thereof) between the ability to perform intelligence and the <em>necessary</em> possession or activity of intelligence is not self-evident, however Turing’s goal was to illustrate the assumptions involved in this perspective and establish an alternative observational narrative of ‘thinking’ that broke the philosophical paralysis on machine thought and consciousness." note="Nath, 2020; Turing, 1950." %} Given the importance of understanding thinking in the modern artificial intelligence world, and the way that these tools reflect back onto us a mechanised version of our own skills and behaviours, a test for this question is incredibly valuable as a matter beyond simple armchair theorisation, affecting ethics, epistemology, theory of mind, and beyond. On this, I argue that the test is highly effective for breaking the philosophical deadlock Turing observed and it can be carefully dissected to reveal further questions to be asked around the nature of intelligence and behaviour in all contexts. It is highly generative. Importantly to my application of Butler within this framework, I argue that both Butler and Turing are exploring the same question of cognitive beingness within incredibly similar contexts, what does it mean to be something if this something is only expressed through doing, and both reach similar conclusions from these positions. Hence my essay will function as an application and exploration of Turing's test, and it’s ‘goodness’ is illustrated in the breadth of this actual exploration.

{% include note.html type="citation" text="The original test involves three participants; A. a man, B. a woman, and C. an interrogator." note="Turing, 1950, pp. 433–434." %} The interrogator communicates with A and B separately exclusively through written messages. In part B, the man is replaced by an AI that communicates through the same channels. The man’s goal is to successfully convince the interrogator that he is a woman, and the woman attempts to help the interrogator figure out who is who. {% include note.html type="citation" text="The interrogator is not, as is often cited, trying to figure out whether the man is human or not." note="Turing, 1950, pp. 433–434." %} Instead both man and machine are pursuing the same tangential goal of misleading the interrogator. The core question is simply, can the computer match or even beat the man in how well he can convince the interrogator he is the woman? If so, then it is reasonable to presume it is thinking. This is in essence the original formulation of the Turing test, and it provides a valuable source of discourse over what intelligence actually is and whether it can be observed through the behaviours that it presumably creates. Importantly, Turing deliberately hedges heavily in his discussion of the topic on whether computers are intelligent or can ‘think’, indeed the entire paper arises out of opposition to this exact premise. This is not a weakness of Turing's argument, Turing never intended to make a test that directly addressed the question of *proving&#x20;*&#x77;hether machines can think, he explicitly rejects this question. Turing’s intention from the beginning is to construct a behavioral test with which we can consider machine internal activity in a new cultural way. This cultural focus is merely implied, but many of his personal beliefs take the frame of cultural predictions rather than philosophical realities. The reason for this cultural focus is likely emergent. His abandonment of the previous stalemated questions that were used to think about AI prompted a new cultural framework for thinking about ‘thinking’ and artificial intelligence, it is possible he was anticipating the effect that his work would have on what people are prepared to call thinking, see {% include note.html type="citation" text="&quot;I believe that at the end of the century the use of words and general educated opinion will have altered so much that one will be able to speak of machines thinking without expecting to be contradicted&quot;." note="Turing, 1950, p. 442." %} In short, {% include note.html type="citation" text="Turing’s theory is a perfect expression of his functionalism, if a computer can perfectly mimic human cognitive behavior across a broad, unrestricted spectrum of conversation, it is a mistake to deny it the status of thinking." note="Turing, 1950; Nath, 2020." %}

This problem can be read from a multitude of angles and in this essay I will focus on the sexual dimension of the original test, while recognising that the wider question is by no means limited to simply the scope of this essay. Additionally, there is a large body of discourse that argues that the form of the original Turing test is merely aesthetic, the goal could be pretending to have authority or be a certain age, or any number of other subjects. But, I argue, we can draw on Butler for her core recognition of performativity in behaviour mostly regardless of the specific example. The point is that both Butler and Turing question the assumption that performed or observable expression implies the presence of some stable beingness behind that expression that is being expressed.

So, as evidenced by Judith Butler’s gender theory there need not be any physical reality behind the performance of gender for it to be obvious or convincing (but there may). Indeed, Butler argues that it is solely this performance of gender that is the reality, that there is nothing behind the curtain. {% include note.html type="citation" text="It is notable that this forms the basis of Turing’s original formulation on guessing sex. Indeed, from Judith butlers statement that &quot;it seems that genders can be neither true nor false, but are only produced as the truth effects of a discourse of primary and stable identity&quot;, it seems to suggest that the idea of the interrogator &quot;getting it right&quot; is already separated from the reality of user A as man or machine by definition; that the performance is all that matters." note="Butler, 1990, p. 174." %} {% include note.html type="citation" text="As in her words &quot;there is no gender identity behind the expressions of gender&quot;, which is reassuring for Turing’s test." note="Butler, 1990, p. 25." %}

To make the connection clearer, the function of gender in Turing’s test fits this structure perfectly. The physical aspect of gender when it comes to expressions of gender that a machine would be lacking is not required to convey the same message about gender itself. Of course this wouldn’t be the case if communication wasn’t done by text communication like Turing suggests, which as an author's note is so close to the humble text message. There is structurally no reason why a machine wouldn’t be able to express gender in the same manner as a human given Butler’s work, or at the very least a machine has no more trouble with expressing gender than a human. This illuminates an important limitation for the Turing Test in that any differentiation between human and machine ability must be caused by something that isn’t accounted for by raw biological difference, which makes sense given Turing's explicit focus on activity; that intelligent communication matters over innate intelligence.

{% include note.html type="citation" text="To return to Turing by itself, the most important thing that the Turing test resolves is the deadlock that theory of mind philosophy was struggling with for centuries between Cartesian Dualism and Behaviourism." note="Nath, 2020; Turing, 1950." %} Dualism struggled to integrate with scientific reality at all without violating the laws of physics, and Behaviourism meant that you had to deny the existence of the mind. Into this situation, Turing’s understanding of theory of mind as an activity finally offered a promising and effective solution to this deadlock following a paradigm shift away from beingness to doingness. {% include note.html type="citation" text="This is why Turing opens CMI by first proposing to consider the question of &quot;Can machines think?&quot; before declaring it absurd as no one could agree on what ‘thinking’ means and suggesting the turing test as a substitute definition." note="Turing, 1950, pp. 433, 442." %}

{% include note.html type="citation" text="As Turing mentions in 6 (4), the second main point of the turing test is that it is not so much a machine test as a human intelligence test." note="Turing, 1950, pp. 445–447." %} {% include note.html type="citation" text="We don’t usually have access to other peoples qualia, we infer their understanding and intelligence from their observed behaviour." note="Nath, 2020; Turing, 1950." %} It is not clear why machines should be treated any differently if given Turing’s premise with text communication they can communicate equlivently. An exception to this, I suppose, would be that Humans have the advantage of familiarity with our own psychological enviroment. We can reason that another human probably experiences the world in a quantitatively similar way.

{% include note.html type="citation" text="For an oppositional perspective, many scholars such as Searle argue that Turing is not actually identifying when something is understood and thinking is occurring." note="Searle, 1980." %} {% include note.html type="citation" text="He does this by imagining a person manipulating Chinese symbols according to a set of rules, producing appropriate responses without understanding Chinese." note="Searle, 1980." %} {% include note.html type="citation" text="From the outside, the responses may appear meaningful, while Searle argues that the person manipulating the symbols does not understand their meaning." note="Searle, 1980." %} This test is thought provoking but in my eyes largely misses the entire point of Turing’s objective, the purpose is that the activity itself is intelligent or thinking, not the system or man inside it. Searle is making a valid point, he is challenging the idea that manipulating symbols according to rules can be sufficient for semantic understanding, but this is adding an assumption that there must still be something that must understand rather than Turing’s point that the construction of a computer is irrelevant, they all perform the same work. The intelligence or thinking is not within the rules, or the person, or the computer as a whole, but in the process of computing. {% include note.html type="citation" text="Turing wrote: &quot;I do not wish to give the impression that I think there is no mystery about consciousness…But I do not think these mysteries necessarily need to be solved before we can answer the question with which we are concerned in this paper&quot;." note="Turing, 1950, p. 447." %}

When I first started this course, I recall the first thing I contributed in class was on the question “What is a computer?” to which I responded that “a computer is that which computes. What computing is however…” and I didn’t know from there. But the fundamental realisation that the Turing test provides is that, similarly to Butler’s Gender Trouble, the point of consideration should not be the objects themselves, but the processes that are occurring on top of them. In my view, the turing test is highly effective at what it sets out to do. {% include note.html type="citation" text="This is not to provide absolute proof that a specific machine thinks, that is impossible following Gettier’s objection of chance." note="Gettier, 1963." %} However, in fulfilling its job of breaking the deadlock by creating a new framework to explore and establishing a way to determine what machine intelligence could look like, then it is highly effective. It is not as powerful as a test as it is a thought experiment to understand computing itself and what machine intelligence and intelligence in general could look like. But yes, especially given its place in history, the Turing test is a good test.
